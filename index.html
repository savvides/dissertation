Teaching Science Lab Safety · ASU PhD (2018) & 2026 Reanalysis VR Didn't Beat Desktop — and the “Presence” Effect Was a Mirage

Eight years after defending my dissertation (n = 108), I reanalyzed the study and uncovered an ecological fallacy.

  • +4.1 vs. +3.8 VR vs. desktop learning gain No detectable VR edge (d = 0.23, p = .33)
  • 8.5× Simulation gain over video Means +3.9 vs. +0.5; 21 of 37 video learners gained ≤ 0
  • r = .51 → n.s. Presence–learning link Steep across all 108; vanishes inside every group

01 / 06 The Experiment

Two groups used an interactive lab. One watched a video.

I randomly assigned 108 university students to three conditions: a Labster lab-safety simulation in a VR headset, the same simulation on a desktop computer, or a CrashCourse video and handout.

Notice the asymmetry: VR and desktop isolated immersion on identical content; the control group changed both interactivity and material.

Study protocol

Standalone Lenovo Mirage Solo headset (High, n = 35), desktop PC (Medium, n = 36), and video with a rules handout (Low, n = 37). Sessions averaged 25.9 min in VR vs. 16.2 min in control; 92 students completed the one-week follow-up.

Learning gain by condition (10-question test) All 108 post − pre scores, with group means and 95% confidence intervals.

02 / 06 The Outcome

Simulations beat video 8.5×, but VR didn't beat desktop

On a 10-question lab-safety knowledge test, simulation learners averaged +3.9 points and held that lead a week later. In the video group, 21 of 37 learners gained nothing or lost points.

Yet more immersion didn't produce a detectably larger learning gain (+4.1 vs. +3.8; d = 0.23, p = .33). Why didn't the headset pull ahead?

Outcome scale

Learning gain is posttest minus pretest on a 10-item safety exam (range −2 to +7). Simulation groups averaged +3.92 vs. +0.46 for video (d > 2.4), though content and session length also differed.

Pooled across all 108, presence tracks learning Each mark is one participant (jittered slightly). Line: overall OLS trend.

03 / 06 The 2018 Theory

In the pooled data, “being there” looked like the cause

The original hypothesis centered on presence — the sense of “being there.” In theory, richer immersion deepens presence, and presence drives learning.

Plot all 108 students, and the slope confirms it: presence strongly predicts gain (r = .51, p < .001). In 2018, that looked like the mechanism.

Within each condition, the link fades Solid lines: trend inside each group (none significant). Dashed: all 108 pooled.

04 / 06 The Twist

Inside each group, the presence effect vanishes

A 2026 reanalysis exposed an ecological fallacy, a group-level pattern mistaken for an individual-level one. Color the dots by condition, and the steep diagonal fractures.

Within each group, presence barely tracks learning (r = .16 for VR, −.19 for desktop, and .12 for video; none statistically significant). Inside any condition, feeling present shows no reliable link to learning.

Three groups separated against the pooled diagonal Same 108 learners in separate panels. Dashed line: pooled r = .51 illusion.

05 / 06 Why It Happened

Pull the groups apart, and the mirage collapses

Side by side, the three panels reveal what built the pooled diagonal. Asking video watchers whether they felt inside a virtual room broke the yardstick: 14 of 37 (38%) scored zero on presence, anchoring the bottom left; 58 of 71 simulation learners clustered at the top right.

Inside each panel, the slope flattens — and desktop tilts negative. Participant #88 watched the video, scored 60/60 on presence, and lost a point.

Instrument validity

The Witmer & Singer questionnaire (0–60) measured presence. Its items assume an interactive environment, so video watchers piled up at 0 (median 6, vs. 46 for VR and 43.5 for desktop).

Cognitive load across all 108 learners (0–12) Two NASA-TLX items. Every group shared a median of 2; lines show means and 95% CIs.

06 / 06 An Open Question

Headset friction didn't explain the tie

One counterargument remained: an unfamiliar headset might have added cognitive load and held VR back. VR averaged higher load (2.9, vs. 2.2 for desktop and 2.0 for video), pulled up by four VR outliers at 7–12; every desktop and video learner stayed at 6 or below.

Yet every group shared a median of 2, and the differences weren't statistically significant. Inside VR, learners with higher load didn't learn less (r = .14, not significant). Novelty cost is a hypothesis this study raises, not one it confirms.

Inside those four VR outliers

The four VR participants reporting cognitive load ≥ 7 averaged a +4.3 gain (vs. +4.1 for VR overall), including participant #50, who hit 12/12 load and gained +4.

Measure the learner, not the bucket

Eight years later, I found a fallacy hiding in my own data. In 2018, I reported that presence predicted how well participants scored after training. The 2026 reanalysis showed that the link lived between conditions, not within them: inside each condition, it vanished. The dataset hadn't changed. My eyes had.

Three lessons follow from what held up in the numbers:

01 · Learning Buyers

Test the laptop before buying headsets

Desktop simulations delivered +3.8 vs. VR's +4.1 without headset overhead. Make immersion beat a solid desktop build before paying for headsets.

02 · Analysts & Researchers

Plot inside each group before claiming a cause

A strong pooled correlation can deceive. Check within-group slopes and control-group floors before claiming a mediator works.

03 · VR Designers

Onboard the controls before measuring learning

Although this study couldn't prove novelty cost, one cheap precaution follows: teach movement and navigation first. Familiarity precedes flow.
