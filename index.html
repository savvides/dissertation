01 — The Experiment

108 Participants, Three Worlds

What happens when you teach lab safety inside a virtual world? To find out, 108 university students were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: a Labster lab-safety simulation in a VR headset, the same simulation on a desktop computer, or a video-and-handout control. The two simulation groups saw identical content; the control group learned the same safety topic from a CrashCourse video and a handout.

Would more immersion mean more learning?

Both simulations far outpaced the video Average points gained on a 10-question safety test. Lines show 95% confidence intervals.

02 — The Promise

Simulations Work

The initial results were encouraging. On a 10-question lab-safety knowledge test, both simulation groups scored significantly higher than the control group, right after the session and again a week later. Whether it ran in a headset or on a desktop, the simulation group learned far more than the group that watched the video.

But here's the catch: the VR group did not significantly outperform the desktop group. More immersion didn't produce a detectably larger learning gain. Why not?

03 — The Deeper Question

But Why?

The original hypothesis centered on presence — the psychological sense of "being there" inside a virtual environment. The theory was elegant: higher immersion induces stronger presence, and stronger presence drives deeper learning.

When we plot every participant's presence score against their learning gains, the data seems to support this. A clear positive trend emerges across all 108 participants. Case closed?

Within each group, the link fades Solid lines: trend within each condition. Dashed: all 108 pooled.

04 — The Twist

Look Closer

Not quite. A 2026 reanalysis found something the original study missed: an ecological fallacy, a group-level pattern mistaken for an individual-level one. The overall correlation between presence and learning was real, but it came from differences between the groups, not from anything happening within individuals.

When we color each dot by its experimental condition, the picture changes. Within each group, presence barely tracks learning (r = .16 for VR, −.19 for desktop and .12 for video; none statistically significant). The overall trend comes from the gap between groups: both simulation groups reported far more presence and learned far more than the video group. Inside any one group, there was no reliable link between feeling present and learning.

05 — An Open Question

The Price of Novelty?

If presence wasn't the mechanism, why didn't VR pull ahead of desktop? One candidate is cognitive load. The VR group reported the highest average load (2.9, vs. 2.2 for desktop and 2.0 for video), which fits the idea that an unfamiliar headset costs attention.

But the differences weren't statistically significant, and inside the VR group, people who reported more load didn't learn less (r = .14, not significant). Novelty cost is a hypothesis this study raises, not one it confirms.

What I Found

Eight years later, I found a fallacy hiding in my own data. In 2018, I reported that presence predicted how well participants scored after training. The 2026 reanalysis showed that relationship lived between the groups, not within them: inside each condition, it vanishes. The dataset hadn't changed. My eyes had.

If you're designing learning technology, the takeaway is narrower than the theoretical implication. A strong group-level pattern can be seductive and false. Ask whether you're measuring the thing or the bucket. Ask whether the relationship survives at the individual level. Here, it didn't.

If you're specifically building VR for learning, one cheap precaution follows, even though this study couldn't prove the mechanism: train people on the interface before you measure what they learned with it. Attention spent on "where do I look, how do I move" is attention not spent on the content. Familiarity precedes flow.
